Fire departments find out too late.

Most systems detect a fire after it ignites. Warnings cover whole regions, so nobody knows which patch of forest is dangerous on a given day.

No pre-positioning

Trucks, tankers and crews cannot be staged near danger zones ahead of time.

Regional, not local

Existing warnings cover broad areas, not the specific grid zone at risk.

No lead time

Communities get little or no notice to prepare or evacuate.

What does what. Every risk score comes from a deterministic weighted formula (see “How the risk score is calculated” below). It is not a trained machine-learning model. Groq, a language model, is connected on the server and only explains those scores in plain language. It never sets or changes a score.

How the risk score is calculated.

A fixed, inspectable formula, not a trained model. The same code scores the demo data and any CSV you upload.

Inputs and weights

Calculation

  1. Each input is turned into a 0 to 1 danger value using the band in the table.
  2. The five values are combined as a weighted sum. The weights add up to 100%.
  3. The sum is rescaled to 0 to 100 and rounded, giving the risk score.
  4. The score maps to low, medium or high risk. High risk raises an alert.

Worked example

What Groq AI does (and does not do)

  • The deterministic engine produces every risk score. Groq never sets, changes or double-checks a score.
  • Groq reads the current forecast and alert information the app sends it, then writes zone explanations, executive briefings and answers to operational questions.
  • Each request carries the forecast numbers and the active data source (demonstration or uploaded). The prompts forbid inventing data, fires or accuracy figures.
  • The API key stays on the server. If Groq is unavailable you see an error message and the forecast keeps working.

What kind of data is behind a number?

Limitations

  • Data quality and coverage. A score is only as good as its inputs. The 24-zone grid is illustrative, and gaps or sensor errors in your data carry straight into the score.
  • Weather forecast uncertainty. Forecasts lose accuracy toward Day 7, and so do the scores built on them.
  • Satellite revisits. Sentinel-2 passes over a location about every 5 days, and cloud can delay it, so vegetation dryness can be several days old.
  • Risk is not detection. A high score means conditions favour fire. It does not mean a fire has started, and fires started by people are hard to predict from environmental data.
  • Local validation is required. The weights are expert-set and have not been tested against real outcomes. Validate locally before any operational use.
